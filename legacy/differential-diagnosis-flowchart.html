Breastfeeding Problem Workup

A differential-diagnosis map for the parent–infant dyad, from presenting complaint to working diagnosis.

Orientation

How to use this map

This is a pattern-recognition aid, not a protocol. It organises the common causes of breastfeeding difficulty into four complaint-led pathways and points each working diagnosis to its discriminating features and first management steps. Work through it in order.

  1. Gather the standard data set — a dyad history, a full feed observation, and the infant’s growth and output. The next section lists what to capture.
  2. Screen for red flags. If any are present, act on them before continuing any pathway.
  3. Enter the pathway that matches the predominant complaint. Expect overlap — run a second pathway whenever the picture calls for it.
  4. Follow the flowchart to a working diagnosis (A1–D8), then read the matching table row beneath it.
  5. Re-observe a feed after any intervention. If the problem does not respond as expected, revise the diagnosis rather than repeating the treatment.

Flowchart symbols

  • Finding or clinical state
  • Decision point
  • Working diagnosis — see the matching table row
  • Likely normal — reassure and follow up

Every presentation

Core assessment of the dyad

The same four inputs underpin every pathway. Collect them before you branch — most diagnostic errors here come from skipping the feed observation or the growth data.

Parent / lactation history

Breast changes in pregnancy and the days after birth; degree of engorgement. Prior breast or chest surgery, radiation, trauma, piercing. Previous breastfeeding experience and supply. Endocrine history — thyroid, PCOS, diabetes, subfertility, retained placenta or PPH. Current medications and contraception. Pain: site, timing, quality, triggers. Mood and support. Nipple and breast appearance on exam.

Birth & infant history

Gestation, birth weight and route, instrumental delivery, birth injury, Apgars, resuscitation, intrapartum opioids or magnesium. Nursery or SCBU stay, tube or bottle feeding, suctioning or intubation. Stool and void pattern, meconium passage, jaundice. Tone, alertness, craniofacial or genetic concerns.

The feed observation — do this every time

Pre-feed state and cues. Positioning and head–body alignment. Gape, seal, lip flange, chin contact, areolar coverage. Rhythmic suck–swallow with audible swallowing and appropriate pauses. Parent’s pain score during and after. Nipple shape on release. Infant behaviour at the end — content, still rooting, or asleep early. Time at breast.

Objective data

Serial weights plotted on a growth chart — percentile crossing, percent of birth weight, grams per day. Pre- and post-feed weights where available. Wet and dirty nappies per 24 hours; feed frequency; any supplement volume. Bilirubin where indicated; in the parent, TSH, prolactin, FBC or CRP when the history points there.

Screen first

Red flags — exclude before entering a pathway

Infant
  • Weight loss ≥ 10% of birth weight, or not back to birth weight by day 10–14
  • Fewer than 6 wet or 3 dirty nappies per day after day 4
  • Lethargy, weak cry, poor tone, or reluctance to feed
  • Signs of dehydration or hypoglycaemia; deepening jaundice, or jaundice with poor feeding
  • Apnoea, cyanosis, choking, or respiratory distress with feeds
  • Bilious or projectile vomiting
Parent
  • Fever with rigors or looking unwell, not improving on conservative care
  • A fluctuant or rapidly enlarging breast mass; skin breakdown, necrosis, or suspected abscess
  • Erythema with peau d’orange, or a fixed mass not resolving on antibiotics — exclude inflammatory carcinoma
  • Grouped vesicular nipple lesions (HSV)
  • Severe unremitting pain
  • Thoughts of self-harm or of harming the baby

Action: ensure the infant is fed now (expressed milk or supplement), stabilise or admit as needed, protect supply with regular expression, and refer urgently to the appropriate team.

Start here

Master triage

Route by the predominant complaint, after the history, the feed observation, and the growth review. Then work the indicated pathway.

Pathway A

Nipple & breast pain

Pain is the commonest reason a breastfeeding parent seeks help and the one most often mislabelled. Anchor the differential on when the pain occurs, the appearance of the nipple and areola, and whether there is a colour change.

IDWorking diagnosisDiscriminating featuresFirst steps
A1 Positioning & attachment problem Pain worst at latch, eases with flow. Shallow gape, inturned lips, chin off the breast, clicking; nipple lipstick-shaped or striped after the feed. Often some nipple trauma. Hands-on latch correction, asymmetric/exaggerated latch, laid-back and other positions. Review within a few days. If unresolved despite good technique, reassess for oral restriction (A2).
A2 Suboptimal latch with infant oral restriction Persistent shallow latch despite correction. Restricted tongue elevation or extension, cupped or heart-shaped tip, high or bubble palate, retrognathia. Poor transfer, ongoing nipple trauma, maternal pain. Structured oral-function assessment (e.g. Hazelbaker) by an IBCLC or feeding team. Positional compensation. Frenotomy only for demonstrated functional restriction — not for appearance alone.
A3 Nipple trauma / fissure Cracks, abrasions, bruising or scab; bleeding. Pain localises to the wound. An entry point for infection if it fails to heal. Correct the cause (latch, pump fit). Moist wound healing. If not improving in 48–72 h, honey-crusted, or with spreading erythema → swab and treat empirically for S. aureus (consider MRSA).
A4 Nipple / areolar dermatitis Itch, burning, scale, well-demarcated erythema. Flare after a new cream, breast pad or detergent (contact); or a history of atopy or psoriasis — look for plaques elsewhere. Remove candidate allergens (lanolin, fragrances, topical antibiotics). Short course of a topical corticosteroid. Patch testing if recurrent. Treat any secondary infection.
A5 Bleb (milk blister) White or yellow dot at the nipple tip, pinpoint pain, tender duct proximally. Often recurrent at one spot, on a background of hyperlactation. Warm soak then feed. Do not routinely unroof. Address the upstream inflammation and oversupply (as D2). Persistent or recurrent → review technique and supply; some use lecithin.
A6 HSV or bullous impetigo Grouped vesicles, punched-out ulcers, honey crust; prodromal tingling; systemic upset in primary HSV. HSV: do not feed from the affected breast until lesions crust; express and discard. Systemic aciclovir. Neonatal HSV is life-threatening — urgent paediatric advice. Impetigo: topical/oral antibiotics per severity.
A7 Raynaud’s phenomenon of the nipple Nipple blanches then turns blue/red — biphasic or triphasic. Triggered by cold or the end of a feed. Throbbing between feeds, relieved by warmth. May have Raynaud’s elsewhere or a precipitating drug (β-blocker, fluconazole, combined pill). Warmth immediately after feeds; avoid cold, caffeine and nicotine. Correct any latch component (vasospasm is often provoked by compression). Nifedipine if severe and persistent.
A8 Deep breast pain with a normal exam Bilateral, burning or shooting; skin and nipple exam normal. Historically attributed to “ductal candidiasis”, for which the evidence is weak and contested. Re-verify latch and pump-flange size. Treat vasospasm (as A7). Consider mammary dysbiosis and persistent/functional pain (central sensitisation) with a mood screen. Empirical antifungal or antibiotic treatment is not recommended without corroborating signs (infant oral thrush, satellite areolar rash).

Pathway B

Low milk supply

The first task is to separate perceived from measured insufficiency — most referrals are the former. When supply is genuinely low, the fork is whether milk ever came in abundantly (a primary or lactogenesis II problem) or came in and then fell (a removal, drug, surgical or endocrine problem).

IDWorking diagnosisDiscriminating featuresFirst steps
B1 Perceived insufficient milk supply Weight gain and output normal, growth tracking. Concern driven by soft breasts, short or frequent feeds, cluster feeding, or a baby who “always wants more”. Reassure with the objective data. Teach normal infant behaviour and hunger cues. Safety-net advice on output and weight. Arrange a follow-up weight check.
B2 Insufficient glandular tissue (mammary hypoplasia) Little breast change in pregnancy or postpartum; minimal engorgement. Tubular shape, wide (>3–4 cm) intermammary gap, marked asymmetry, bulbous areolae. Low supply from the outset. Optimise removal; supplement to protect the infant. Galactagogue trial. At-breast supplementer. Counsel that supply may remain partial. Involve an IBCLC.
B3 Delayed lactogenesis II Copious milk later than ~72 h. Risk factors: caesarean or long labour, PPH, retained placenta, diabetes or GDM, obesity, PCOS, thyroid disease, severe stress, theca-lutein cysts. Frequent effective removal (feed plus express) 8–12×/day; skin-to-skin. Treat the contributing factor. Supplement as needed while supply builds. Re-weigh in 24–48 h.
B4 Ineffective or infrequent milk removal Established supply now falling. Poor latch or tongue restriction, scheduled or capped feeds, unnecessary top-ups, dummy overuse, a sleepy or late-preterm baby, nipple-shield misuse, return to work, night weaning. Increase feed and expression frequency; ensure effective transfer. Wean unnecessary supplements gradually to a written plan. Correct latch. Power-pumping.
B5 Medication effect or new pregnancy Temporal link to combined hormonal contraception, pseudoephedrine, high-dose progestins, ergometrine, cabergoline or bromocriptine, or heavy alcohol use. Or a supply drop coinciding with a new pregnancy. Stop or switch the offending drug where possible (progestogen-only or non-hormonal contraception; avoid decongestants). If pregnant, counsel that some decline is expected.
B6 Prior breast surgery, radiation or trauma Reduction (especially free-nipple graft), augmentation, chest-wall radiation, major burn or degloving, previous large abscess. Periareolar incisions; altered nipple sensation. Assess for ductal and nerve disruption. Protect infant intake; supplement as needed. Supplementer plus galactagogue trial. Give realistic counselling on likely capacity.
B7 Retained placental fragments Low supply with ongoing lochia, subinvolution, or secondary PPH. Plateau despite good technique. Elevated βhCG; retained products on ultrasound. Obstetric referral. Supply commonly recovers after evacuation. Maintain removal in the meantime.
B8 Endocrine cause Hypothyroidism (fatigue, cold intolerance). PCOS (may over- or under-produce). Hyperprolactinaemia. Sheehan’s (failure to lactate with amenorrhoea and fatigue). Poorly controlled diabetes. Check TSH and free T4, prolactin, and glucose. Treat the disorder; endocrinology referral if abnormal. Continue removal throughout.

Pathway C

Latch difficulty & breast refusal

Distinguish a sudden strike in a baby who fed well from a persistent problem present since birth. Then work through infant structure, infant neuromotor status and medical illness, and finally the dyad mechanics — refusal is frequently more than one of these at once.

IDWorking diagnosisDiscriminating featuresFirst steps
C1 Nursing strike Sudden refusal in a baby who fed well. Recent URTI or otitis media (pain on sucking), oral thrush, hand-foot-and-mouth, teething, a bite–startle reaction, a drop in supply, a strong let-down, distraction (~3–4 months), or a routine or stress change. Skin-to-skin, offer when sleepy or in motion, dark quiet room, treat pain or illness. Express to protect supply and feed the milk. Usually resolves within days.
C2 Oral-structural cause Ankyloglossia with impaired function (poor cup, clicking, spill, slides off). Submucous or overt cleft (bifid uvula, nasal regurgitation). High or bubble palate. Micro- or retrognathia (Pierre Robin). Macroglossia (Beckwith-Wiedemann, congenital hypothyroidism, trisomy 21). Choanal atresia (cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying). Full oral exam. Feeding team, and ENT, plastics or genetics as indicated. Positional strategies; cleft plate or appliance. Frenotomy for functional restriction only.
C3 Neuromotor dysfunction or immaturity Hypotonia (trisomy 21, prematurity, hypothyroidism, neuromuscular disease), hypertonia, or birth injury (cephalohaematoma, clavicle fracture, torticollis, facial-nerve palsy, brachial plexus injury, HIE). Late-preterm or early-term: disorganised suck–swallow–breathe, tires quickly. Support tone and head position; external pacing; breast compression; shorter, more frequent feeds. Physiotherapy or OT. Paediatric review. Protect intake.
C4 Cardiorespiratory disease Tachypnoea, sweating or colour change with feeds, tiring after a few minutes, poor endurance, faltering growth. Murmur, hepatomegaly. Paediatric or cardiology assessment; SpO₂. Pace feeds and allow breaks; fortify or supplement to reduce the work of feeding.
C5 GORD / CMPA / eosinophilic oesophagitis Arching, crying and pulling off mid-feed; feed-associated pain. Eczema, blood or mucus in the stool, family atopy (CMPA). For suspected CMPA, a 2–4 week trial of maternal dairy (± soy) exclusion. Positioning. Reserve acid suppression for confirmed pathological reflux. Paediatric or allergy referral.
C6 Oral aversion History of intubation, deep suctioning, an NG tube, or force-feeding. Turns the head, clamps, or gags as the breast approaches; otherwise well and growing on alternative feeds. Gentle, non-pressured re-introduction; responsive feeding; feeding therapy (SLT or OT). Never force. Treat any underlying pain.
C7 Engorgement or nipple shape Areola too firm to draw in when engorged. Flat or inverted nipples. Nipple or breast large relative to a small or preterm mouth. Reverse-pressure softening and hand expression before latching. Laid-back positioning. Nipple-everting techniques. A shield as a short-term bridge, with follow-up.
C8 Oversupply & overactive let-down Choking, gulping, clamping, pulling off as milk sprays. Gassy; frequent explosive green frothy stools; rapid weight gain. One breast per feed or block feeding. Laid-back position. Brief pre-latch expression. Review and reduce any unnecessary pumping.
C9 Low-supply frustration Fusses, latches then pulls off crying, more settled on the bottle. Poor transfer and slow weight gain. Work up as Pathway B. Supplement (ideally at the breast) while building supply. Latch and transfer support.
C10 Teat or dummy preference Refusal emerges after regular bottle use. Roots and latches but will not sustain; prefers a fast teat flow. Paced bottle-feeding with a slow teat. Reduce dummy use. Feed at early cues. At-breast supplementer. Skin-to-skin.

Pathway D

Inflammatory breast & lump

Framed as the mastitis spectrum (ABM Protocol #36, 2022): a continuum from engorgement and ductal inflammation through inflammatory and bacterial mastitis to phlegmon and abscess. Most of the ladder is managed conservatively; the important branch is knowing when to add antibiotics, when to image, and when to stop treating and biopsy.

IDWorking diagnosisDiscriminating featuresFirst steps
D1 Physiological engorgement Bilateral, days 2–5. Both breasts diffusely full, firm, tender, warm; sometimes a brief low-grade fever. No focal wedge, no systemic illness. Feed frequently and responsively. Cold packs between feeds. Reverse-pressure softening. NSAIDs. Avoid aggressive pumping.
D2 Ductal narrowing / inflammation (“plugged duct”) A focal firm tender segment, sometimes with overlying pink, and no or minimal systemic symptoms. Usually on a background of hyperlactation. Keep to the normal feeding schedule — do not try to “empty” the breast. Ice, NSAIDs, gentle lymphatic drainage toward the axilla, therapeutic ultrasound. Avoid deep massage, heat, extra pumping, and saline soaks.
D3 Inflammatory mastitis A firm erythematous tender wedge plus systemic features — fever ≥38.5 °C, rigors, myalgia, tachycardia, malaise. Conservative measures as D2, NSAIDs, adequate analgesia, rest. Review at 24 h. Antibiotics are not first-line if symptoms are <24 h and improving.
D4 Bacterial mastitis Inflammatory mastitis not improving in 24–48 h, or worsening. A cracked nipple with spreading cellulitis. Systemically unwell or septic. Anti-staphylococcal antibiotics (e.g. dicloxacillin or flucloxacillin, or cephalexin; cover MRSA per local rates). Send a milk culture. Continue feeding or expressing. Escalate if no response.
D5 Phlegmon An ill-defined, firm, enlarging mass in an area of preceding mastitis, worsening despite appropriate antibiotics, with no drainable collection on ultrasound. Ultrasound to exclude abscess. Continue or broaden antibiotics (IV if unwell). Do not incise. Watchful management with breast-surgery involvement.
D6 Breast abscess A fluctuant, well-circumscribed, exquisitely tender mass, sometimes pointing. A discrete collection on ultrasound. Ultrasound-guided needle aspiration (repeat as needed) or drainage; culture; antibiotics. Keep removing milk. Surgical referral.
D7 Galactocele A smooth, non-tender, cystic swelling, often after weaning or in a stable position. Milk-density fluid on ultrasound; milky aspirate. Confirm on ultrasound. Aspirate if symptomatic or for diagnosis. Recurrence is common while lactating.
D8 Inflammatory breast cancer — must exclude Erythema, oedema and peau d’orange not settling on antibiotics. Skin thickening, nipple retraction, a hard fixed mass, matted axillary nodes. Onset outside the typical postpartum window. Urgent breast imaging (ultrasound and mammography) and core biopsy of skin and any mass. Do not repeat antibiotic courses without imaging. Refer on a 2-week cancer pathway.

Non-lactational inflammatory breast disease sits outside this spectrum: periductal mastitis (central, associated with smoking, may form a fistula), idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (a biopsy diagnosis), and tuberculous mastitis where endemic.
